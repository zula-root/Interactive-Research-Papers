Caffeine After Studying Borota et al.  ·  a 10-minute read

Nature Neuroscience 17  ·  Borota, Murray, Keceli et al., Johns Hopkins

Timing of caffeine matters when studying

Can a coffee after you learn help you remember tomorrow?

Caffeine is known to make people more alert, which could help them learn. Whether it also strengthens memories once learning is over was unclear, because earlier studies gave caffeine before the learning began. This team gave it afterwards and tested memory the next day. Caffeine improved one specific kind of memory: telling apart things that are similar but not identical.

No background in neuroscience is assumed. Each section stands alone.

Start here 2 min  ·  the core idea

Memories keep forming after you stop learning

A new memory starts out fragile. Over the following hours, and especially during sleep, the brain stabilises it, a process called consolidation. Anything that acts during that window can strengthen or weaken what you keep.

Caffeine taken before studying changes alertness, attention and mood while you learn, so any memory benefit could come from any of those. Giving it only after the learning is over rules them out. If memory still improves, caffeine must be acting on consolidation.

The participants were people who rarely consumed caffeine. They looked at pictures of everyday objects, then took a 200 mg tablet or a placebo five minutes later. Saliva samples confirmed the caffeine levels. The next day, they were tested.

Try this right now

Think of where you last parked a car or locked a bike in a place you use often. Now try to recall where you left it the time before.

The two memories are nearly identical, and they blur. Keeping similar memories separate is called pattern separation, and it is the ability this study found caffeine improved.

The hours after learning are part of learning.

Finding 01 2 min  ·  timing

After studying, not before the test

To check that caffeine was not simply sharpening people up on test day, the team ran a version in which caffeine was given an hour before the test instead. That produced no benefit. The effect depended on caffeine being present while the memory was still settling. Move the dose along the timeline.

Finding 02 3 min  ·  the test

Telling similar things apart

On the second day, participants saw three kinds of picture: ones they had seen before, new ones, and lures that closely resembled earlier pictures. They labelled each as old, new or similar. Both groups recognised old pictures equally well. The caffeine group was better at calling the lures "similar" instead of mistaking them for the originals. Finding 03 2 min  ·  dose

More is not better

The team repeated the experiment at three doses. At 100 mg there was no measurable benefit. At 200 mg the improvement appeared. At 300 mg it was about the same as at 200. For comparison, a typical cup of brewed coffee contains around 95 mg. Set the dose.

In conclusion 2 min  ·  what follows from it

A small, specific boost

The paper shows that caffeine can act on memory after learning, not only on attention during it. The effect was narrow and measured in a particular group of people.

In practice

A coffee after the session, not at night

Consolidation also depends on sleep, and caffeine late in the day disrupts it. A dose after an afternoon session fits the study; one after a late-night session may cost more than it gives. See Sleep Rinses the Brain.

Who it applies to

Light caffeine users only

Participants were chosen because they rarely consumed caffeine. Regular coffee drinkers build tolerance, and the study did not test whether the effect survives it.

Evidence since

Promising, not settled

This is one lab's well-controlled result on one task. It has not been widely replicated, and it showed no benefit for ordinary recognition. Treat it as a reason to pay attention to timing, not as a study aid.
