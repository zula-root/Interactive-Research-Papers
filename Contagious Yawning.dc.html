Front. Evol. Neurosci. 3, 3  ·  Andrew Gallup & Omar Eldakar, Princeton & Arizona

Are yawns contagious?

Why does the weather change how often we catch each other's yawns?

Yawning is usually read as a sign of boredom or tiredness. A competing idea holds that it cools the brain: the deep breath and wide stretch of the jaw draw in air and increase blood flow, exchanging warm blood for cooler. If so, yawning should only help when the air is cooler than the body. Andrew Gallup and Omar Eldakar tested this on the streets of Tucson, Arizona, in winter and in summer. This page condenses the paper into an 8-minute read.

Start here 2 min  ·  the core idea

A radiator for the brain

The brain works best within a narrow temperature range, and it generates a lot of heat. Earlier studies by Gallup and others found that brain temperature rises slightly before a yawn and falls after it. The proposed mechanism is simple: a yawn pulls in a large breath of air and pumps blood through the skull, carrying heat away.

That idea makes a testable prediction. Cooling by air only works if the air is cooler than you. When it is as hot as the body, around 37°C, a yawn would bring in no relief, so people should yawn less.

Tucson, Arizona offers both conditions within one year: mild winters and early summers at or above body temperature.

Finding 01 2 min  ·  winter and summer

Almost twice as many yawns in winter

The researchers approached pedestrians outdoors in winter, at around 22°C, and again in early summer, at around 37°C. Each looked at pictures of people yawning and reported whether they yawned themselves. In winter 45% did; in summer, 24%. Choose a season and show the photos.

Finding 02 2 min  ·  time outside

Minutes in the heat switch yawning off

Participants also said how long they had been outdoors before being tested. In summer, prolonged exposure to air at or above body temperature was linked to less yawning. In winter the pattern reversed: people who had spent longer in the mild air were more likely to yawn. Drag the slider and switch season.

Finding 03 2 min  ·  the thermal window

Yawning has a sweet spot

The Tucson results raised a follow-up question: does yawning also fall off when it is very cold, when the brain needs no extra cooling? A 2014 study in Vienna by the same group found that it does. Contagious yawning peaked at moderate temperatures around 20°C and dropped in both heat and cold. Notably, Vienna's yawning peak fell in summer, the opposite season to Tucson's, because Vienna's summers are mild. Turn the thermostat.

In conclusion 1 min  ·  what follows from it

A yawn may be more than boredom

This was a small field study, but it was the first to show that human yawning varies with season, as the cooling hypothesis predicts.

Try it yourself

Cool your forehead

An earlier lab study by Gallup and Gallup (2007) found that people holding a cold pack to their forehead, or breathing only through the nose, caught far fewer yawns from a video than people who did neither.

Why contagious?

Cooling, shared

The authors speculate that catching yawns may synchronise alertness in a group: if one person's brain is warming, others in the same conditions probably are too.

Limits

Self-reported, and still debated

Participants reported their own yawns, and season can affect other things such as sleep and mood. Other researchers link contagious yawning mainly to social closeness and empathy. The two explanations may both be partly right.
