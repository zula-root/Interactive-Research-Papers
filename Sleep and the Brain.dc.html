Does sleep clean the brain?

Why would an animal spend a third of its life unconscious?

Every animal studied so far sleeps, despite the obvious risk of lying still and unaware. In 2013 Maiken Nedergaard's lab offered a concrete reason: during sleep, the space between brain cells expands and cerebrospinal fluid flushes metabolic waste out of the tissue, including the amyloid-β protein associated with Alzheimer's disease. This page condenses the paper into a 10-minute read.

No background in neuroscience is assumed. Each section stands alone.

Start here 2 min  ·  the core idea

A city that washes its streets at night

Most of the body removes cellular waste through the lymphatic system, a network of vessels that drains fluid from tissue. The brain has no lymphatic vessels inside it, and it is the most metabolically active organ we have. How it disposes of its waste was an open question for a long time.

In 2012 the same lab described the glymphatic system: cerebrospinal fluid enters the brain along the outside of arteries, washes through the gaps between cells, and leaves along veins, carrying waste with it. This paper asked when that system does most of its work. The answer was during sleep.

The experiments were done in mice, using dyes injected into cerebrospinal fluid and live imaging through a small window in the skull.

Try this right now

Picture a kitchen sponge squeezed in your fist, then held under a running tap. Nothing much gets through. Open your hand and the sponge expands; water rushes through it and carries the grit out.

Your brain tissue behaves roughly like that sponge. Awake, the cells are swollen and the gaps are tight. Asleep, the cells shrink slightly and fluid can pass between them.

Sleep changes the physical structure of the brain, and that change is what lets it clean itself.

Finding 01 2 min  ·  the space between cells

Asleep, the gaps between brain cells grow by about 60%

The team measured the interstitial space, the fluid-filled gaps between cells, in the same mice awake and asleep. Awake, it made up about 14% of brain volume. Asleep, about 23%. Cerebrospinal fluid flowed into the tissue far more readily as a result. Toggle the state and watch the fluid move.

Finding 02 2 min  ·  clearing amyloid-β

Waste leaves the sleeping brain about twice as fast

To test whether wider channels mattered, the researchers injected labelled amyloid-β into the brain and measured how quickly it disappeared. In sleeping and anaesthetised mice it was cleared about twice as fast as in awake ones. Release the same dose into both brains and compare.

Finding 03 2 min  ·  the switch

Noradrenaline holds the channels shut

Noradrenaline is the neurotransmitter that keeps the brain alert; its levels fall during sleep. When the researchers applied drugs that block noradrenaline's receptors to the brains of awake mice, the interstitial space expanded almost to sleeping levels. In conclusion 2 min  ·  what follows, and what is still argued

Sleep as maintenance

The paper gave sleep a physical, measurable function. It also opened a debate that is still running.

Health

Rest is not idle time

If clearance depends on sleep, then chronically short sleep is not only tiredness. It may mean waste accumulating in tissue that has no other way to remove it.

Alzheimer's

A plausible link, not a proven cause

Amyloid-β build-up is a hallmark of Alzheimer's, and poor sleep is common years before diagnosis. A 2018 human brain-imaging study found that one night without sleep raised amyloid-β levels in healthy adults. Whether better sleep prevents disease remains untested.

The debate

Not everyone gets the same result

In 2024 a group at Imperial College London reported in Nature Neuroscience that brain clearance in mice fell during sleep and anaesthesia. The methods differ, and the field has not settled which measurements are right. The mouse-to-human step is also unproven.
