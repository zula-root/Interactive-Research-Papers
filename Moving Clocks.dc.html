Annalen der Physik 17, 891  ·  Albert Einstein, Bern

Einstein's slow clocks

If light always travels at the same speed, what has to give?

In 1905 a 26-year-old patent clerk published "On the Electrodynamics of Moving Bodies." Starting from two simple assumptions, Einstein showed that time and length are not fixed quantities: they depend on how fast you are moving relative to what you measure. This is special relativity. This page condenses the paper into a 10-minute read.

No physics background is assumed. The paper itself reasons with trains, clocks and light signals, and so does this page.

Start here 2 min  ·  the core idea

Two rules that seem to contradict each other

Einstein took two principles as given. First, the laws of physics are the same for everyone moving at a steady speed; no experiment inside a smoothly moving train can tell you whether it is moving. Second, light in a vacuum always travels at the same speed, about 300,000 km per second, however fast its source is moving.

Together these break common sense. If you chase a beam of light, the second rule says it still recedes from you at full speed. The only way both rules hold is if your measurements of distance and time change with your motion. Speed is distance divided by time; if the speed of light is fixed, distance and time must adjust.

The paper works out exactly how they adjust. The effects are tiny at everyday speeds and large near the speed of light.

Try this right now

Remember the last time you sat on a train in a station and the train beside you pulled away. For a moment you could not tell which one was moving.

That confusion is the first rule. Neither train has a privileged claim to being at rest. Einstein's insight was to take that literally, and then add light.

There is no universal clock. Each observer carries their own.

Finding 01 2 min  ·  simultaneity

“At the same time” depends on who is watching

A lamp flashes in the exact middle of a moving carriage. For a passenger, the light travels equal distances at equal speed and reaches both ends together. For someone on the platform, the rear wall moves towards the light and the front wall moves away from it, so the light reaches the rear first. Both accounts are correct. Section 1 of the paper builds on this point. Flash the lamp, then switch viewpoints.

Finding 02 2 min  ·  time dilation

A moving clock ticks slower

Imagine a clock that ticks each time a pulse of light bounces between two mirrors. Set one clock moving. Seen from outside, its light follows a longer, diagonal path. Since light cannot go faster to compensate, each tick takes longer. The slowdown factor, written γ (gamma), depends only on speed. Drag the slider and count the ticks.

Finding 03 2 min  ·  length contraction

Moving things shrink, and particles from space prove it

The same factor γ shortens a moving object along its direction of travel. A real test arrived decades later. Cosmic rays create muons about 15 km up. A muon lives on average 2.2 millionths of a second, long enough to travel only about 660 m, yet large numbers reach the ground. From our view, the muon's clock runs slow. From the muon's view, the atmosphere is contracted. Set the muon's speed.

In conclusion 2 min  ·  where it shows up today

You use relativity every day

Special relativity is among the most thoroughly tested ideas in science. Its consequences are built into ordinary technology.

Navigation

GPS corrects for it

GPS satellites move fast enough that their clocks lose about 7 microseconds a day to time dilation. Gravity, from Einstein's later general theory, speeds them up by about 45. Uncorrected, positions would drift by kilometres each day.

Energy

E = mc² followed months later

A three-page follow-up in the same year showed that mass and energy are equivalent. It explains why the Sun shines and how nuclear reactors work.

Method

Taking simple rules seriously

The paper has no references and almost no experimental data. It accepts two principles and follows them to their conclusions, however strange. That approach became a model for theoretical physics.
